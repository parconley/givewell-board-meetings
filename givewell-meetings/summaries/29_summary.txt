RELATIONSHIP TO EFFECTIVE ALTRUISM MOVEMENT

The board engaged in an extensive 21-minute discussion of GiveWell's relationship to the Effective Altruism (EA) movement. This represented an important strategic conversation about positioning, community engagement, brand identity, and organizational priorities as both GiveWell and the broader EA movement had grown and evolved.

The Effective Altruism movement, which coalesced in the early 2010s around principles of using evidence and reason to determine how to benefit others as much as possible, had significant overlap with GiveWell's mission and audience. Many EA community members were GiveWell donors and advocates. GiveWell's research was frequently cited in EA discussions and served as a foundational resource for people new to effective giving.

However, the relationship was complex. GiveWell predated the formal EA movement, having been founded in 2007. While aligned on core principles of evidence-based charitable giving, GiveWell maintained organizational independence and distinct identity. The EA movement encompassed broader concerns beyond charitable giving, including career choice, animal welfare, existential risk reduction, and other cause areas that GiveWell did not directly address.

Discussion topics likely included: How closely should GiveWell identify with the EA movement versus maintaining independent brand identity? What obligations, if any, did GiveWell have to support EA community building and infrastructure? How should GiveWell engage with EA-identified donors versus the broader donor audience? What risks existed from being too closely associated with EA if the movement faced criticism or controversy? How could GiveWell benefit from EA networks and thought leadership while maintaining research independence?

The length of this discussion (21 minutes) indicated substantive board engagement with strategic positioning questions. As the EA movement gained prominence and sophistication, clarifying GiveWell's relationship became increasingly important for branding, fundraising, community engagement, and strategic planning.

The discussion likely considered different stakeholder perspectives: long-time GiveWell supporters who predated EA movement might prefer emphasis on GiveWell's independent track record; EA community members might want stronger identification and mutual support; major donors might have varying levels of EA affinity; and mainstream philanthropists might view EA association as helpful or potentially alienating depending on their backgrounds and values.

GIVEWELL AND OPEN PHILANTHROPY PROJECT RELATIONSHIP

The board spent 32 minutes discussing the relationship between GiveWell and the Open Philanthropy Project, making this the longest and presumably most complex topic of the meeting. This extended the conversation from the June 2015 board meeting where formal roles, responsibilities, and financial arrangements were established.

Possible discussion topics included: How was the formalized relationship working in practice several months after establishment? Were the 50% Open Philanthropy/20% GiveWell funding arrangements from Good Ventures functioning smoothly? How clearly could external audiences distinguish between GiveWell and Open Philanthropy Project? What tensions existed between allocating staff time and attention to traditional GiveWell work versus Open Philanthropy research? Were governance structures adequate for the increasingly substantial Open Philanthropy Project activity?

The conversation likely addressed operational challenges emerging from the joint structure: coordination of hiring and staff allocation; budgeting and expense attribution between the two entities; intellectual property and publication decisions; website and communications strategies; and balancing the needs and priorities of Good Ventures (Open Philanthropy's primary partner) with GiveWell's obligations to individual donors and institutional supporters.

Strategic questions probably included: Should Open Philanthropy Project eventually become a fully separate organization? If so, what timeline made sense? What were the benefits and costs of continued integration versus separation? How did staff identify themselves and their work - as GiveWell employees doing Open Philanthropy research, or as Open Philanthropy researchers employed by GiveWell? What long-term vision did stakeholders have for the relationship?

The discussion may have explored how the two research streams were influencing each other: Was Open Philanthropy research methodology improving GiveWell's traditional charity evaluation? Was GiveWell's rigorous process discipline benefiting Open Philanthropy investigations? Were there opportunities for cross-fertilization and shared learning?

Talent management and career development implications likely received attention: How did researchers choose between or move between GiveWell and Open Philanthropy work? Were there distinct skill sets and professional development paths for each? How could the organization attract talent to both areas while maintaining clear organizational structure?

The 32-minute discussion length suggested ongoing complexity and evolution in the relationship rather than simple implementation of previously established policies. The board was actively grappling with the challenges of running what were increasingly functioning as two related but distinct organizations under shared organizational structure and leadership.

ORGANIZATIONAL CONTEXT AND STRATEGIC SIGNIFICANCE

This October 2015 meeting occurred during a period of rapid expansion and increasing organizational complexity. GiveWell had grown from a small startup to a substantial research organization with multiple distinct initiatives. The traditional charity evaluation work continued to grow in influence and sophistication. The Open Philanthropy Project had evolved from exploratory research to active grantmaking influencing tens of millions of dollars annually. Staff numbered in the dozens rather than the handful of employees from just a few years earlier.

The extensive discussions of relationships - to the Effective Altruism movement and to the Open Philanthropy Project - reflected organizational identity questions natural for a maturing organization. Early-stage organizations focus primarily on execution and survival. As organizations succeed and grow, they must increasingly address questions of positioning, branding, stakeholder relationships, and strategic direction.

The EA movement discussion revealed tensions between independence and community: GiveWell valued its distinct identity and track record but also benefited from EA networks and thought leadership. Finding the right balance required careful consideration of benefits and risks of closer identification versus arms-length engagement.

The Open Philanthropy discussion revealed the challenges of hybrid structures: joint initiatives can leverage complementary strengths and resources but create governance complexity, coordination challenges, and potential for mission drift or divided attention. The ongoing board attention to this relationship suggested it remained an active area of strategic management rather than a settled question.

The relatively focused agenda (three main topics over 60 minutes) enabled substantive discussion rather than superficial coverage of many items. This reflected mature board governance: identifying the most important strategic questions and dedicating adequate time for thorough exploration rather than perfunctory approvals of predetermined conclusions.
