The Clear Fund Board of Directors convened on April 23, 2008, to conduct a comprehensive retrospective analysis of GiveWell's startup year and establish strategic direction for 2008. This meeting represented a critical inflection point where the organization transitioned from proof-of-concept to sustainable operational model, addressing lessons learned while defining metrics, governance structures, and growth strategies.

Startup Year Review: Research Quality and Methodology

The board evaluated performance against four inaugural year goals: conducting quality research, creating a quality product, drawing attention to the product and underlying ideas, and building toward organizational maturity and self-sustainability.

Research effectiveness assessment revealed mixed but ultimately positive results. Charity participation exceeded expectations, though specific numerical details were to be inserted regarding charities contacted versus applicants. The grant application process successfully enabled GiveWell to answer "what do you do" questions with unprecedented concreteness unavailable from website information alone. However, outcomes data proved largely publicly available for strongest performers (KIPP, NFP, HOPE), with organizations like PSI, Year Up, St. Nick's, and Interplast providing non-public outcomes data. Most other charities lacked outcomes data entirely, suggesting fundamental gaps in sector-wide evaluation capacity rather than access limitations.

Timeline analysis indicated Round 1 recommendations required approximately 4 weeks for finalist selection and writeups. Round 2 consumed August through March (8 months) for 5 causes, including significant disruptions from the December 20-January 20 astroturfing crisis period, with staff working unsustainably at 110 hours per person per week for 2 months. Fair assessment suggested each cause required approximately 2 months at current scale and methodology, revealing initial underestimation of time commitments that prevented fulfilling formal obligations to applicants.

Value-added assessment varied significantly across causes. Cause 1 (saving lives in Africa) demonstrated that radically different approaches yielded radically different costs, with clear divergence between proven/simple interventions (bednets, condoms, hospitals) and unproven approaches (health education), representing substantial value addition. Cause 2 (economic opportunity in Africa) yielded practically no value addition, with staff shocked at information scarcity. Cause 3 (early childhood care NYC) identified one exceptional charity (Nurse-Family Partnership) implementing proven methodology while no alternatives demonstrated evidence of effectiveness. Cause 4 (K-12 education NYC) determined most methods ineffective while identifying one likely effective model (KIPP). Cause 5 (employment assistance NYC) revealed significant effectiveness variations due to divergent approaches and populations, though staff questioned whether optimal selection occurred.

Critically, staff concluded they added more value through across-cause comparisons than within-cause evaluations. Understanding that Cause 5 programs cost $20,000-25,000 per enrollee for potentially ineffective programs while health programs could save lives for $1,000-5,000 represented transformational insight. This led to the fundamental conclusion that causes were too narrow, forcing suboptimal fund allocation while remaining too broad for true "apples to apples" comparisons—a goal now deemed entirely futile.

Product Development and Public Reception

Website creation cost approximately $5,000, with quality judged by December's 50,000 hits. Detailed metrics including bounce rates, average time on site (differentiated between Adwords traffic and New York Times readers), survey completion numbers, permission-to-contact rates, user quality evaluations, and donation totals were to be benchmarked against industry standards, demonstrating systematic approach to performance measurement.

Attention-generation yielded decidedly mixed results. Positive outcomes included substantial sector buzz, with Chronicle of Philanthropy cover feature and increased discussion of needs for resources beyond Charity Navigator appearing on blogs, in Hewlett Foundation discussions, and Wall Street Journal coverage. The New York Times article provided best general exposure, focusing on staff personalities alongside product, becoming the #3 most emailed article and generating considerable web traffic.

Negative outcomes centered on the astroturfing fiasco, where poor staff judgment created negative exposure, burned bridges with specific online communities, and raised nonprofit sector questions about organizational ethics. Additionally, many stakeholders expressed antipathy toward GiveWell due to controllable factors (organizational tone) and uncontrollable characteristics (youth, inexperience, for-profit backgrounds). Staff acknowledged their personalities served as both assets and liabilities, generating attention while creating problems, leading to intentional toning down of public persona.

Organizational development achievements included incorporation, 501(c)(3) status acquisition, bylaw establishment, accounting procedure implementation, Board of Directors construction, and greatly expanded contact networks. Shortcomings included failure to establish clear Board roles and expectations, inadequate organizational metrics, and astroturfing incidents potentially damaging sector standing and partnership capacity.

2008 Strategic Framework: Four Core Goals

Goal 1: Increase Money Moved. The board identified increasing "money moved"—defined as sum of Clear Fund direct grants plus individual donations to recommended charities primarily based on GiveWell recommendations—as the paramount 2008 objective. Long-term organizational viability depended on moving money at scales justifying overhead costs, providing empirical validation of research value to donors.

Staff proposed fundamental restructuring away from the traditional "United Way / Robin Hood model" of pooling donations for re-granting toward soliciting formal donor commitments to donate to GiveWell-recommended charities with donor choice retained. This created three distinct donor offerings: (1) unrestricted gifts to GiveWell for operating expenses, targeted at major/institutional donors and core supporters interested in philanthropy improvement generally; (2) managed funds where donors cede charity choice to GiveWell, to be de-emphasized initially; (3) the "GiveWell Pledge Fund," a donor-advised fund allowing donors to commit funds for future distribution to GiveWell-recommended charities while retaining advisory rights and final charity selection, subject to Donor Deadlines. The third option became the primary fundraising focus, removing unnecessary obstacles to donor commitment while providing concrete, measurable impact tracking.

This approach acknowledged drawbacks including operating two separate fundraising streams (operating expenses wouldn't scale automatically with money moved) and sacrificing pooled donation benefits like funding dedicated evaluations. However, with sufficient unrestricted funds to operate through 2008, successful GiveWell Pledge generation would create compelling cases to major funders by demonstrating precise leverage on operational investments.

Staff proposed hiring an experienced Development Director at competitive salary, acknowledging current staff lacked fundraising expertise. Such hire would establish long-term growth plans, execute development strategy, assume major public communications roles, potentially share or assume Executive Director responsibilities, address current staff weaknesses, and provide straightforward evaluation through clear metrics. Preliminary fundraising strategies included contacting approximately 1,000 website survey respondents who granted contact permission, targeting specific industries and companies for presentations, and organizing fundraising events leveraging existing contacts to attract new prospects.

Goal 2: Enhanced Research Quality. While expanding reach constituted the primary 2008 goal, continued progress on maximizing charitable impact through quality research remained essential. The large grant methodology enabling information collection through application processes remained optimal despite no alternative approaches yielding comparable information within budget and timeline constraints.

However, significant methodology modifications were planned. Cause breadth would increase dramatically, abandoning futile attempts at "apples to apples" comparisons in favor of grouping charities by likely donor interest. Conversations with donors and survey data indicated potential supporters held relatively broad interests—for example, developing-world health interest typically correlated with developing-world economic empowerment and education interest. This justified much broader 2008 cause definitions than 2007's narrow categories.

Number of causes would decrease from five simultaneous causes to one at a time (unless donor demand justified additional staff hiring), eliminating major logistical problems that kept applicants on hold for weeks while reviewing other causes, often requiring quick turnarounds when staff returned attention. Five completed causes provided sufficient proof of concept, making simultaneous multi-cause research unnecessary.

Grant size and structure would increase from $25,000-40,000 one-time unrestricted grants to promises of at least $100,000+ for at least one charity per cause, based on feedback from charities declining applications. Five declined charities cited they would reconsider for $100,000+ grants, with two naming different thresholds ($75,000, $200,000). With unrestricted funds sufficient for one $100,000 grant if operating expenses remained stable, this became feasible even if money moved primarily consisted of GiveWell Pledges.

Prioritized causes in order were: (1) Developing-world direct aid (health, microfinance, economic assistance, education, abandoned children), best fitting potential donors per survey data with clear value-addition opportunities; (2) Global warming and catastrophe aversion, second-best donor fit though staff less familiar with investigation approaches; (3) Disease research, with ambivalence about value-addition capacity; (4) U.S. youth development (K-12 education, college scholarships, extracurricular activities, early childhood programs); (5) U.S. veterans support.

Goal 3: Engage Critical Thinkers. Critical discussion of GiveWell research came from limited sources: staff, directors reviewing writeups and discussing grant decisions, specific experts consulted (Professor Dean Karlan of Poverty Action Lab, Charles Buice of Tiger Foundation, Professor Richard Murnane on education, relevant graduate students), volunteer editors, and occasional blog commenters (primarily four individuals: Michael Vassar, Carl Shulman, Phil Steinmeyer, Dario Amodei). Expanding this community would improve research quality, enhance donor credibility, clarify website layout and content, and identify potential future staff from committed critical thinkers. Strategies included proactively building cause-specific advisory boards with experienced accomplished professionals and holding periodic research discussion meetings with interested parties from GiveWell's volunteer list.

Goal 4: Organizational Development. Board expansion sought members providing expertise on studied issues (likely placed on cause-specific advisory boards), perspectives from diverse backgrounds (fundraising, grantmaking, on-the-ground charity work, management), and greater time commitments enabling lower-level decision-making participation.

Metrics formalization established "money moved" as primary indicator, measured as sum of direct GiveWell grants to recommended charities (including GiveWell Pledges) plus individuals' donations to recommended charities largely based on GiveWell recommendations (partially measurable through website donation link tracking via Network for Good). Long-term target aimed for money moved equaling at least 9x operating expenses (excluding grants), considered generally acceptable overhead levels. 2008 tentative target was $500,000 moved (subject to revision after development professional consultations), compared to 2007's $170,000 ($140,000 grants, $30,000 through GiveWell.net).

Secondary metric tracked "impact on global discussion" qualitatively, examining whether philanthropy conversation aligned with GiveWell principles by archiving representative discussions from publications (WSJ, NYT, Chronicle of Philanthropy) and blogs, measuring frequency of principle engagement and agreement. Long-term target fostered active public discussion about charity activities and effectiveness evidence. 2008 target sought downward trends in favorable mentions of administrative expense ratios as primary charity evaluation metrics.
