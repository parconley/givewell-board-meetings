The Clear Fund Board of Directors convened on March 4, 2008, to make critical grant decisions for two cause areas—early childhood care (Cause 3) and K-12 education (Cause 4)—while establishing strategic direction for 2008. This meeting represented the organization's first major grantmaking decisions following the January 2008 ethics crisis, demonstrating GiveWell's continued operational capacity and commitment to evidence-based philanthropy.

Cause 3: Early Childhood Care - Analysis and Decision

The board evaluated options for supporting early childhood development, primarily comparing the Nurse-Family Partnership (NFP) model against Head Start-style programs. Staff presented extensive research demonstrating the relative merits of each approach.

The Nurse-Family Partnership model employs trained registered nurses to visit low-income, single mothers beginning early in pregnancy, addressing prenatal health (nutrition, reducing alcohol/tobacco/drug use, obtaining prenatal care), child care (creating safe and supportive home environments), and personal life management (birth spacing, education, and employment). Three methodologically strong randomized controlled trials with moderate attrition and blind data collection provided evidence of effectiveness across diverse populations of low-income first-time mothers.

The Elmira County NY trial followed families for 15 years, finding statistically significant effects on children's disciplinary records. The Memphis trial, with 3-year and 6-year follow-ups (the latter published in 2004), found visited mothers' children scored superiorly on vocabulary, arithmetic, and mental processing composites. The Denver trial's 4-year followup published in 2004 showed superior scores on language and behavioral adaptation. All three trials demonstrated statistically significant effects on mothers' birth spacing and two found welfare usage effects attributable to birth spacing improvements.

NFP distinguished itself from similar programs using paraprofessionals rather than registered nurses. The Denver study provided preliminary evidence that paraprofessional effects were approximately half as large as those achieved by registered nurses. Program costs ranged from $10,000 per family (per NFP's website) to approximately $14,000 for the full 2.75-year program based on sample startup budgets of $500,000 annually for 100 families. The national organization's role focused on training, support, and oversight rather than direct funding of partner sites, with costs of approximately $10 million for 75,000 families projected for the following year.

Staff characterized NFP as exceptional among all examined organizations across any cause for its clarity of strategy, commitment to replicating proven models, and ability to continue learning through ongoing randomized trials and comprehensive data collection from all sites. The organization maintained extremely thorough data on demographics, attrition, implementation (visit numbers and content), and broad outcomes including maternal smoking/alcohol consumption, life situations, and children's test scores.

Head Start-style programs, providing 3-4 hours daily of daycare to 4-5 year-old children from families below the poverty line (with 90% of participants below poverty line), included educational programming, nutrition and health services, and often parental components. Universal Pre-K in New York provided additional funds, oversight, and generally extended care from partial to full days. Review of methodologically rigorous studies showed mixed evidence, with 15 of 24 studies showing statistically significant advantages as of grade 3 or later on measures including high school graduation, achievement tests, special education, or grade retention. Programs cost $10,000-12,000 per child served annually, with New York State Head Start Centers receiving $8,800 per child enrolled annually.

Staff recommended awarding the Cause 3 grant to Nurse-Family Partnership, citing: (a) greater confidence the grant would fund "doing more of what works" given limited understanding of how additional funds would affect preschool applicants; (b) NFP's impressive organizational alignment with GiveWell's principles and capacity to continue expanding knowledge of effective interventions.

Cause 4: K-12 Education - Comprehensive Analysis and Recommendations

Staff presented detailed analyses of three primary organizations: KIPP, Teach for America, and Children's Scholarship Fund, with KIPP receiving the strongest recommendation.

KIPP operated a network of 50+ charter schools nationwide. The KIPP Foundation recruited and trained principals, negotiated with states to start schools, and provided startup assistance. KIPP NYC raised supplemental private funds and operated programs supporting KIPP students' education. The model's core value proposition centered on founders personally identifying exceptional principals, providing them administrative support through a Shared Services team while maintaining autonomy, principals' focus on teacher recruitment, and extended school hours.

Evidence of KIPP's effectiveness came from multiple sources. Two quasi-experimental studies—one of KIPP Diamond Academy showing 5th and 6th grade students significantly outperforming matched comparison groups, another of KIPP Ujima studying four cohorts from 2002-05 finding significant test score improvements for students remaining at KIPP—supported the model's impact. Staff analyzed all publicly available test data covering 23 of 57 existing KIPP schools across 8 of 18 states with KIPP presence. Results showed students enrolled in KIPP schools significantly improved test scores relative to neighboring schools, with gains maintained throughout middle school.

Critical to the analysis was addressing student attrition and addition. Between 10-25% of students enrolling at KIPP did not progress from 5th to 6th grade (either leaving or being retained). Students leaving KIPP scored significantly worse than those staying, roughly equivalent to district schools. Students arriving scored significantly better than district average but lower than existing KIPP students. Staff modeling indicated that even under relatively high attrition scenarios (20-30%), the KIPP effect could not be explained entirely by student turnover, providing confidence in genuine program impact.

KIPP schools received funding from per-pupil allocations ($11,000 per student), state and federal grants for new schools, KIPP Foundation support, and private donations. Based on detailed budget information from four NYC KIPP schools, staff estimated costs at $5,000-10,000 per student beyond per-pupil funding.

Teach for America recruited, trained, and placed recent college graduates in low-income public schools. A rigorous Mathematica study using randomized controlled design found TFA teachers had small but statistically significant impact on math scores with no impact on reading scores, covering 100 teachers across 6 urban and rural regions. TFA had increased corps members from 2,100 to 2,400 between 2005-2006 with plans to reach 4,200 by 2010, spending $15,000-20,000 per teacher placed.

Children's Scholarship Fund provided privately subsidized vouchers enabling low-income children to attend private schools. Rigorous studies showed no statistically significant effect on academic performance for the population as a whole, with possible effects for African-American students—though even this finding was contested. CSF spent approximately $30 million providing scholarships to 25,000 children, with average tuition at recipient schools of $3,000 annually and CSF providing approximately $1,500 per student.

Staff recommended KIPP for Cause 4, acknowledging Teach for America and Children's Scholarship Fund as reasonable alternatives.

2008 Strategic Planning and Organizational Development

Staff presented preliminary plans for 2008 emphasizing three broad goals: development (increasing "money moved" by GiveWell), research (improving understanding of best ways to help people), and administration (building mature organizational governance).

A critical strategic shift involved focusing on obtaining formal donor commitments to donate to GiveWell-recommended charities (with donors retaining charity choice) rather than raising money for re-granting. This approach would offer donors three options: donating directly to GiveWell for research activities, donating to GiveWell for "managed" funds with charity choice ceded to staff, or committing funds in special accounts for future donation to recommended charities in donors' fields of interest. Staff proposed focusing fundraising on the third option, raising the first only from major/institutional donors and core supporters, and de-emphasizing the second.

Staff proposed hiring an experienced, talented Development Director at competitive salary, acknowledging current staff lacked fundraising experience and expertise. Such a hire would set long-term growth plans, execute development strategy, take major roles in public communications, potentially share or assume the Executive Director role, and be relatively straightforward to evaluate with clear metrics for assessing return on investment.

For research scope, staff recommended focusing on one broad cause at a time (unless donor demand justified additional staff), taking donor preferences heavily into account. The preferred next cause was developing-world direct aid, including health, microfinance and economic assistance, education, and abandoned children, based on popularity in online surveys and staff's concrete sense of investigation approaches and value-addition opportunities. Alternative causes under consideration included global warming and catastrophe aversion, and extremely broad analysis publishing basic analysis of many options rather than thorough organization-level analysis of few.

This represented a significant shift from the startup year's narrow cause definitions toward broader causes, driven by frustration with allocating one grant per cause rather than directing more resources to causes with stronger organizations and better cost-effectiveness. Staff proposed potentially adopting more "top-down" research approaches, starting with independent research on publicly published evidence of effectiveness before targeting specific organizations.

Administrative priorities included recruiting additional Board members with diverse experience and perspectives, forming cause-specific Advisory Boards, formalizing metrics and public goals, establishing policies and procedures for Board and staff, and producing thorough retrospective analysis of the first year including survey data analysis.

The meeting agenda allocated two hours for grant decisions and thirty minutes for preliminary 2008 planning, with agreement to complete formal review of the first year and detailed 2008 plan within approximately one month, scheduling the next board meeting for early April 2008.
